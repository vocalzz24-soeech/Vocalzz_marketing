Stammering (हकलाना) at college or work: what helps, what doesn't, and when to see someone

23 November 2026 · By the Vocalzz team

"Mera naam bolte waqt hi atak jaata hoon."

It's one of the most common things teenagers and adults who stammer say. Not the hard words in a presentation. Their own name. The one word they've said more than any other.

If that's you, a few things are worth knowing before anything else. Stammering (stuttering, हकलाना) is common. It has nothing to do with intelligence or confidence as a person. And it usually changes from day to day, which is exactly why it feels so unpredictable: fine with friends on Saturday, stuck on the phone with a bank on Monday morning.

What's actually happening when you get stuck

There are roughly three things people notice. Repeating a sound ("b-b-bus"), stretching it ("sssorry"), or the one most adults hate, the block, where the mouth is ready and nothing comes out. Blocks are usually where the tension builds. The harder you push, the tighter everything gets, and the longer the moment feels.

So most of what helps is about tension, not about "speaking properly".

Things that tend to help

Ease into the first sound. A lot of stammering lives at the start of a word. Let a little air out first and start the word softly, almost lazily. It feels odd for the first week. It stops feeling odd.

Touch, don't press. On p, b, t, d and k, your lips or tongue have to close. Close them lightly. People who stammer often press them together hard without realising it.

Pause before you have to. A pause you choose sounds calm. A pause that happens to you sounds like a block. Short planned pauses between phrases give you that control, and honestly, listeners hardly notice them.

Stay with the listener. Looking away during a stammer is a reflex, and it usually makes the moment bigger. Keeping eye contact, even when it's uncomfortable, tends to shrink it.

Rehearse the situations you dread, out loud. Not in your head. Say your introduction, the first line of a phone call, the "tell me about yourself" answer, to a mirror or a voice recorder. Then try it somewhere low-stakes, like ordering at a café, before the interview.

Some people also find it easier to just say it upfront: "I stammer a bit, give me a second." It's not for everyone, but when it works, the pressure drops for both people.

And the boring one that matters: tired, rushed or anxious days are usually worse. Sleep and a few minutes of slack before an important conversation genuinely help.

The habits that quietly make it worse

Swapping words is the big one. It works in the moment, which is why everyone does it. But over months the list of "dangerous" words tends to grow, and so does the planning that goes into every sentence.

Avoiding situations works the same way. Not ordering, staying quiet in meetings, letting calls go to voicemail. Each one makes the next one harder.

Forcing through a block and rushing to get the sentence over with both add tension, which is the thing you're trying to reduce.

When is it worth seeing a speech therapist?

When stammering is deciding things for you. If you're not answering in class, not applying for the job that needs presentations, choosing what to eat by how easy it is to say, that's a good time. Same if there's a viva, interview or wedding speech coming up and you want to feel prepared.

We won't promise a "cure", and you should be wary of anyone who does. What therapy can do, for a lot of people, is make speaking easier and the stuck moments shorter and less frightening. It's never too late to start.

If you're the parent of a teen who stammers

Don't finish their words, and try not to say "slow down" or "take a breath", even kindly. Give them time and keep looking at them. Ask them what helps. Most teens just want you to listen to what they're saying rather than how they're saying it.
