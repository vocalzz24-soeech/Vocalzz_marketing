10 speech therapy mistakes parents make (and what to do instead)

23 November 2026 · By the Vocalzz team

"Bolo beta, bolo. Mumma bolo."

Every parent of a late talker has said this. Usually more than once a day, usually with a grandparent watching. It comes from love, and so does everything else on this list. None of these are "bad parenting". They're just habits that, without anyone meaning to, can make talking a bit harder for a child who's already finding it hard.

You'll probably recognise a few. Most families do.

1. Waiting because "boys talk late"

Some do. Some children who talk late catch up on their own. The trouble is that nobody can tell which ones by waiting. An assessment either tells you your child is fine, which is a relief, or it gets help started months earlier. Both are better than another six months of wondering.

2. Getting everything before they ask

You know what the point means. Biscuit. Water. The red car. So you hand it over. But if every need is met before your child has to communicate, there's no real reason to try. Next time, hold the biscuit up, look at them, and wait a few seconds. A sound, a gesture, a look at the biscuit and back at you, all of that counts.

3. Turning words into a test

"Say apple. A-P-P-L-E. Come on, say it." Most children respond to this by going quiet. Talking starts to feel like an exam they keep failing. It works better the other way round: you say it, playfully, and don't ask for it back. "Apple! Crunchy apple. Mmm."

4. Correcting every word

When "cat" comes out as "tat", the urge to fix it is strong. Try just saying it back the right way, happily. "Yes! A cat. A big cat." No "wrong", no "say it again". They hear the correct version and they still feel they got it right.

5. Thinking educational videos will do the teaching

Even good videos don't talk back. Children learn to talk through back-and-forth with a person who responds to them. Some screen time isn't a disaster, but swapping part of it for books, songs and floor play makes a real difference, and so does switching off the TV that's on in the background during meals.

6. Talking a lot, and fast

Busy parents talk in long, quick sentences, and there's never a gap. Shorter phrases help. So does slowing down a little and then actually leaving space, a good five seconds, for your child to fill. It feels long. It isn't, for them.

7. Switching to English only

Plenty of families do this, hoping it'll speed things up. Children can learn two languages, and dropping the home language often means parents end up speaking less naturally and less often. Speak the language you're most comfortable in, and speak it a lot. If you're unsure, ask your therapist; it's a fair question.

8. Not checking hearing

It gets skipped more than you'd think. Children learn to speak by hearing, and even a mild or on-and-off hearing problem, say after repeated ear infections, can slow speech down. If speech is late, a hearing check is one of the first things worth doing.

9. Comparing out loud

"Riya is younger and she talks so much." Children understand a lot more than they can say, and they hear this. Notice the small things instead. A new sound. A new gesture. Two words together for the first time. Say it out loud when it happens.

10. Leaving it all to therapy

A session is maybe an hour a week. Your child is with you for most of the other hundred-plus waking hours. That's where progress really happens, in bath time and mealtimes and the walk to the park. Ask your therapist for two or three things to practise at home and fit them into the day. One more thing: don't stop therapy the moment the first words arrive. Check with your therapist first.
